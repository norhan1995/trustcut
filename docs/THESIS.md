# Two-year thesis

Within two years, useful agents will routinely act across organizational boundaries. Identity will become necessary infrastructure, but it will not answer the harder question: is this particular action still authorized, right now?

The dangerous interval is between approval and execution. A budget is spent by a sibling agent, a human withdraws a mandate, or a supplier's authority changes. A valid signature can outlive permission. Trust infrastructure must bind authority, time, scope, and execution—not merely attach a badge to an agent.

TrustCut starts with a narrow wedge: delegated purchasing and operational actions where mistakes have measurable cost. Its reference implementation shows revocation that propagates through a delegation chain, conserved shared budgets, and an execution boundary that rejects stale decisions. Signed receipts make the outcome portable and contestable.

In year one, we would separate organizational key custody, integrate one idempotent purchasing connector, and pilot with teams already deploying agents under manual approval. Success means fewer unauthorized actions and less unnecessary human review, measured against existing controls.

In year two, we would support interoperable credentials and independently operated verifiers, with explicit status freshness and failure guarantees. Revenue would come from enforcement, operational reliability, and audit integrations—not ownership of an agent identity registry.

Our thesis is falsifiable: if existing identity and authorization systems can enforce these changing, chained mandates reliably without extra infrastructure, a new category is unnecessary. If they cannot, the winning trust layer will be the one that can explain—and enforce—when an agent must stop.
